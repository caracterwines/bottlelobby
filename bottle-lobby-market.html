Structurally broken.

The global wine trade is one of the largest and oldest industries in the world — yet it remains fragmented, inefficient, and disconnected. Quality wineries can't find buyers. Distributors can't manage risk. Restaurants can't differentiate. Retailers can't compete. The problem is structural. The opportunity is enormous.

The Structural Problem

Enormous scale.
No functioning infrastructure.

Despite being a multi-hundred-billion-euro global industry, the wine trade has no unified digital infrastructure connecting wineries, distributors, restaurants, and retailers. Every transaction still happens through outdated channels — trade fairs, cold calls, and catalogue browsing — with no data, no transparency, and no efficiency.

The top five global wine producers hold just 15% combined market share. The rest is split among thousands of small producers with no structured path to buyers.
Off-trade channels — retail, specialty shops, online — account for ~77% of global distribution. Most wineries have no direct relationship with this channel at all.
01
Extreme Fragmentation
The top five wine manufacturers globally hold a combined market share of only around 15%. The rest of the market is divided among thousands of producers — most of whom have no structured path to international buyers. A Sauvignon Blanc from Sancerre competes with tens of thousands of other bottles for attention, shelf space, and placement.
02
PROWEIN Is Phasing Out
For decades, trade fairs like PROWEIN in Düsseldorf were the primary channel for wineries to meet distributors. Today, PROWEIN is becoming increasingly inefficient, cost-prohibitive, and is now phasing out. A small winery can spend €20,000–50,000 on a single booth — competing against 60,000+ wines from 70 countries — with no guarantee of a single qualified contact.
03
Post-Pandemic Volatility
Post-pandemic market shifts have forced restaurants and distributors towards safer, simpler wine choices — predominantly single-varietal mono-cépages from established regions. EU wine consumption fell 2.8% in 2024, more than 5% below the five-year average. The market is consolidating around quality, authenticity, and known varieties. Unknown labels are losing placements.
04
No Real-Time Market Data
Distributors must forecast demand months in advance, with no real-time insight into which varieties are trending in which regions. The result: overstock of slow-moving labels, understock of trending ones, and constant margin pressure. White and rosé wines are especially vulnerable, with shelf life creating additional urgency for accurate forecasting that simply doesn't exist today.
05
No Communication Infrastructure
There is no direct communication channel between wineries, distributors, and restaurants. Feedback loops that could improve wines, identify trends, or develop products don't exist. A sommelier in Munich cannot tell a winemaker in Sicily that customers are asking for less oak. The industry operates in silos — and everyone pays the price in slower sales, lower margins, and missed opportunities.
06
The Mono-Cépage Trend
A structural shift is underway: restaurants increasingly prefer single-varietal wines from their traditional regions — Sauvignon Blanc from Sancerre, Syrah from the Rhône, Riesling from the Mosel. Customers know these names. Staff can sell them without lengthy explanation. Cuvées from unknown regions require too much effort. This trend is accelerating, creating a clear opening for a platform that curates and protects exactly these wines.
Rising Trend — Opportunity
Premiumization is accelerating
Consumers globally are trading up — buying fewer bottles but spending more per bottle. The premium and super-premium wine segments are outperforming the overall market. This is particularly strong in Germany, the UK, and emerging markets in Asia. Wineries with quality products and a compelling story are well-positioned — if they can reach the right buyers.
Structural Shift — Urgent
Off-trade dominates: 77% of distribution
Off-trade channels — retail stores, specialty wine shops, online — account for approximately 77% of global wine distribution. Yet most wineries have no direct relationship with retailers and rely entirely on distributors as intermediaries. Retail is the fastest-growing channel, yet the least served by existing B2B infrastructure. The gap between winery and shelf has never been wider.

Stakeholder Analysis

Four players.
Four broken systems.

Each stakeholder in the wine trade faces a distinct set of structural challenges. Understanding these problems — in depth — is what Bottle Lobby was built to solve.

The Winery Problem

Quality wine producers with significant export potential are systematically locked out of international markets. The channels that once existed — trade fairs, agents, cold outreach — have become too costly, too slow, or too inefficient. Meanwhile, the major importers already carry selections from virtually every prominent wine region. There is simply no functioning channel left.

No data on target markets

Most international wineries lack insight into national consumption patterns, regional preferences, trending varieties, and pricing benchmarks in their target export markets. Entering Germany, Austria, or Switzerland blind — without knowing which varieties restaurants are currently requesting — leads to wasted investment and failed market entries.

No structured route to importers

The story is always the same: there is no functioning channel to reach the right people. Wineries send emails that go unanswered, attend trade fairs where their booth is lost among 60,000 others, and spend years trying to find a single credible importer in a new market. What they need is not logistics — they need an agent or platform that actively creates demand.

PROWEIN is phasing out

For decades, PROWEIN was the primary B2B channel for market entry. Today it costs €20,000–50,000 per booth, returns are difficult to measure, and the event itself is losing relevance. With events like PROWEIN becoming increasingly inefficient and cost-prohibitive, wineries are left with few viable options.

Distributors won't take risks

Most distributors only take on products that are already in demand and effectively pre-sold. They do not invest in marketing or creating demand for a new wine, as doing so carries risk of unsold inventory and uncertain returns. This means that even exceptional, unknown wineries cannot get distribution — because no distributor will invest in building the brand first.

No brand-building infrastructure

What wineries truly need is not just logistics — they need a partner capable of branding, storytelling, press coverage, and navigating regional preferences. Without this, they remain stuck at the gate despite their potential. Most have no marketing budget, no PR contacts, and no way to build the narrative their wines deserve.

The Distributor Problem

Distributors are burdened by choice paralysis, thin margins, and structural risk. Faced with an overwhelming selection of wines and ever-evolving consumer preferences, they have become highly risk-averse — sticking to safe, already-established labels rather than investing in new relationships. The model is built for logistical efficiency, not discovery.

Too many options, no reliable filter

Distributors face thousands of potential new wines every year with no reliable way to assess market fit, demand potential, or regional suitability. The result is an increasingly conservative portfolio strategy — stick to what's already selling, avoid risk, miss opportunities. Even major importers struggle with the sheer volume of choices and the pressure to stay ahead of market movements.

Risk of unsold inventory

The biggest fear: bringing in a wine nobody wants. A key restaurant client switching sommeliers, a trend shifting abruptly, a new competitor undercutting on price — all can leave a distributor holding thousands of bottles they cannot sell. White and rosé wines are especially vulnerable, with limited shelf life adding urgency to an already difficult forecasting challenge.

Razor-thin margins

Margins are already critically thin due to frequent, small-volume orders from restaurateurs and retailers. Distributors must price competitively while covering warehousing, logistics, sales, and marketing costs. Any unsold inventory or price reduction directly erodes profitability. Without pre-validated demand, taking on new labels is a financial gamble most distributors refuse to make.

No real-time market intelligence

Due to the time lag between ordering and delivery, distributors depend on accurate forecasting and up-to-date trend information — which is increasingly difficult in today's volatile market. They have no live data on what restaurants in their region are requesting this quarter, which varieties are trending, or where pricing is heading. Gut feeling replaces evidence.

No demand creation support

Most distributors are only willing to take on products that are already pre-sold. They don't invest in marketing or creating demand for new wines — that's not their business model. But this means only already-famous labels grow. Genuinely good unknown wines never reach their potential. The entire market stagnates around existing brands.

The Restaurant Problem

Restaurants are caught in a complex balancing act between price sensitivity, flexibility, uniqueness, and operational simplicity. They are trapped in their distributor's portfolio — limited variety, price dumping competition, and no flexibility. The wine list next door looks identical to theirs.

Price dumping destroys margins

When wines are widely available and not protected by exclusivity agreements, competitors can undercut prices at will. If a restaurant in Mannheim and one in Heidelberg both source the same Sauvignon Blanc from the same distributor, whoever drops their price first forces the other to follow. Margins collapse. The customer finds the same wine cheaper elsewhere — and the restaurant loses both the margin and the loyalty.

Locked into a single distributor's list

Having multiple suppliers adds stress to procurement and complicates logistics. As a result, most restaurants rely on a single distributor — who may only carry one or two Sauvignon Blancs or Chardonnays. This leads to a homogenization of wine lists across an entire region. Standing out becomes impossible when everyone is ordering from the same catalogue.

Cuvées require too much staff effort

Restaurants often struggle to sell wines from lesser-known wineries, as recommending these requires staff to explain aroma profiles, food pairings, and regional context. Most floor staff lack this knowledge or the time to deliver it. Mono-cépages from traditional regions solve this: customers already know what a Syrah tastes like. Sales become easier, service faster, and recommendations more confident.

No flexibility, no consistency

Post-pandemic volatility has created frequent stock shortages, making it difficult to maintain a stable, curated wine list. Most restaurants no longer commit to full pallets — they need small, frequent deliveries. But traditional distributors are built for bulk logistics, not just-in-time service. Seasonal concepts and changing menus require a supply chain that simply doesn't exist today.

No direct connection to producers

There is no channel for a sommelier to contact a winemaker directly — to ask about a specific vintage, request a tasting for a special event, or provide feedback on a wine. The lack of communication and correspondence between restaurants, distributors, and wineries results in a lack of constructive critique, research, and feedback that would improve the entire supply chain.

The Retail Problem

Independent wine retailers and specialty stores face a structural threat from two directions: supermarkets on one side, online discounters on the other. Without exclusive products, compelling in-store experiences, and direct access to winery stories and materials, the independent retailer has no competitive advantage. The wine on their shelf can be found cheaper online with two clicks.

No product differentiation

Independent retailers carry largely the same wines as supermarkets and online platforms. When the same Sauvignon Blanc is available at Rewe for €8.99 and at the specialty wine shop for €12.50, the customer goes to Rewe. Without exclusive labels or wines that cannot be found elsewhere, the independent retailer competes entirely on price — a war they cannot win against supermarket buying power.

No direct winery access or storytelling tools

Specialty retailers differentiate through knowledge and experience — but they have no direct relationship with producers, no access to high-quality promotional materials, and no way to create compelling in-store events without significant effort and cost. The materials they need — tasting notes, technical sheets, origin stories, producer photography — are scattered across dozens of different sources.

Inflexible ordering — forced to overstock

Traditional distribution models require minimum order quantities that force retailers to overstock or commit capital to inventory they're not certain will sell. For smaller shops with limited storage and cash flow, this is a significant barrier to offering a diverse, high-quality selection. The inability to order small, frequent quantities limits both variety and financial flexibility.

No event infrastructure

In-store wine events — tastings, winemaker evenings, food pairing nights — are the most powerful tool a specialty retailer has to drive sales and build loyalty. But organising these requires contacts with wineries, coordination with distributors, promotional materials, and significant planning effort. Most retailers simply don't have the infrastructure to do this efficiently. Events that could happen monthly happen once a year.

No intelligence on what's trending

Retail buyers have no real-time insight into which wine varieties are trending, which regions are gaining interest, or what consumers are actively searching for. Ordering decisions are made based on gut feeling and last year's sales — not live market data. By the time a trend is visible in retail sales, the window to benefit from it has often already closed.
